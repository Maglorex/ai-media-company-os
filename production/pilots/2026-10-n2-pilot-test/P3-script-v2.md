## S1 — Opening: the contradiction (~0:00–0:55)
**Amaç:** Çelişkiyi kur: çalıştı (sınırlı anlamda) ↔ yıllık ~2M $ ek destek; "neden?" sorusuyla rapora gir.
**Evidence:** C-30, C-14, C-36/Q-06, S1 kapak, C-04/Q-01
**Görsel:** NARA renkli fotoğraf (V-05) · bölge düzeyi sefer haritası (özgün) · NPS alıntı kartı · rapor kapağı + Haziran 1970 damgası (V-03) · özet sayfası cümle vurgusu (V-02).

**VO:**
The NS Savannah was the first nuclear-powered merchant ship. And at sea, she did her job.

In her first three years of commercial service, she made nineteen voyages on regular cargo routes — to the Mediterranean, to northern Europe, and to the Far East. About two hundred and forty thousand miles. A National Park Service history quotes her operators calling her "the most reliable ship we have operating."

But in June 1970, federal auditors sent Congress a report with a different kind of number.

Compared with the support the government paid to keep similar conventional freighters on the same route, the Savannah needed about two million dollars more. Every year.

Not a loss. Extra support.

Why would a ship that kept sailing on regular cargo routes need that much more?

The auditors traced most of the answer to three specific lines in their report.

## S2 — What kind of ship this was (~0:55–1:55)
**Amaç:** Yalnız maliyet hikâyesine hizmet eden bağlam: prototip/gösterim amacı, iki ajans, yolcu alanlarının ticari dönemde kullanılmaması, ticari dönemin başlangıcı.
**Evidence:** C-39, C-18, C-35 (ortak yönetim), C-19, C-20, C-38
**Görsel:** GAO s.4 "prototype" cümlesi · gemi profil şeması: kargo ambarları vs yolcu bölümü (özgün 2D) · sözleşme şeması (Maritime → FAST/American Export; "$1 a year").

**VO:**
To see why, it helps to know what kind of ship this was.

The Savannah was a government project, run jointly by the Maritime Administration and the Atomic Energy Commission, and completed in 1962. In the auditors' words, she was a prototype — built to show the world that the United States wanted to use atomic power for peaceful purposes, and to test whether nuclear power was practical for commercial ships.

So she carried real freight in seven cargo holds — but she also had staterooms for sixty passengers, a lounge and a swimming pool. When she went into commercial service, those passenger spaces went unused. The auditors noted that running them would have cost more than passengers would bring in.

After about three years as a demonstration ship, in August 1965 the government chartered her to a commercial line, American Export Isbrandtsen, for one dollar a year — and put her to work carrying cargo. According to the National Park Service history, the fuel came free of charge.

## S3 — What "worked" means here (~1:55–2:40)
**Amaç:** "Worked" kelimesini kesinleştir; ticari başarı ile karıştırma.
**Evidence:** C-14, C-34, C-36, Q-05
**Görsel:** "19 voyages / ~240,000 miles" sayaç · 1968 yakıt yenileme: 32 demet şeması, 4'ü vurgulu (NPS kaynak notu).

**VO:**
In operation, she held up. When she was refueled in 1968, only four of her thirty-two original fuel elements needed replacing, according to the same history.

So the Savannah worked in a specific sense: she sailed regular commercial routes, and the people running her found her reliable.

Whether running her made financial sense was a separate question. The auditors' report doesn't answer that directly. But it does show, line by line, what she cost.

## S4 — How the auditors counted (~2:40–4:20)
**Amaç:** Yöntem, hariç tutulanlar, karşılaştırma tabanı; ~2M $'ın anlamı.
**Evidence:** C-01, C-16, C-02, C-04, C-05, Q-01
**Görsel:** Net cost denklemi · "not included" kartı (depreciation / nuclear fuel / administration) · GAO s.12 dipnotu vurgusu · iki sütun + "≈ $2M more" köprü etiketi.

**VO:**
The General Accounting Office looked at the ship's commercial years, starting in August 1965.

They measured what's called the net cost: what it cost the government to operate and service the ship, minus the money she earned carrying cargo.

And they were careful about what they left out. The numbers don't include the ship's depreciation, the agency's own administration — or the nuclear fuel.

That last point matters. Nothing in this audit tells us what the uranium itself cost.

Over her first three commercial years, the net cost averaged about two point nine million dollars a year.

On its own, that number doesn't say much. The government also supported ordinary American cargo ships. Because American crews and repairs cost more than those of foreign competitors, the Maritime Administration paid an operating subsidy to make up the difference.

So the auditors compared the Savannah with comparable conventional ships sailing the same trade route. Their finding:

The Savannah cost "about $2 million more a year than the average operating subsidy" paid for those ships.

That two million is not a loss. It's a comparison: how much more support this one ship needed each year than the government already paid to keep a comparable conventional freighter on the same route.

So where did the extra two million come from?

## S5 — The three lines (~4:20–7:05)
**Amaç / Evidence / Görsel:** v1 ile aynı (V-01 tam ekran; C-06, C-08, C-29, C-09, C-30, C-10, C-31, C-32).

**VO:**
The auditors answered that in the summary at the front of their report. Most of the extra cost — about one point nine million dollars a year — came from three things.

Line one: about three hundred thousand dollars a year for nuclear crew training.

Of the ship's sixty-seven crew members, twenty-seven needed at least some nuclear training. Reactor operators spent four to five months in classrooms at the U.S. Merchant Marine Academy, then another four to five months training aboard ship, before taking an exam for a license from the Atomic Energy Commission. By mid-1969, eighty-eight officers and engineers had earned one.

Line two: about four hundred and nine thousand dollars a year for a specially trained nuclear shore staff.

This was a team of about ten people. As the first nuclear-powered merchant ship, the Savannah was watched closely to protect her crew and the communities in the ports she visited. Every port visit took more work and planning than an ordinary freighter needed — surveys, entry and departure arrangements, and in foreign ports, entry arrangements with other governments, worked out together with the Maritime Administration, the Atomic Energy Commission and the State Department.

Line three — the biggest: about one point one six million dollars a year for a nuclear ship-servicing facility.

It was in Galveston, Texas. A pier and work area, plus about twenty-five acres of land kept clear as a safety zone. Inside: a heavy crane, a pool for spent fuel, a servicing barge called the Atomic Servant — and a spare reactor core. By the end of 1969, the government had invested about three point three million dollars in it.

And here is the detail that matters. After her first year of commercial service, the Savannah didn't come back to Galveston for two years. Her annual inspections were done in New York, with staff from the facility traveling there to help.

The facility was there, and paid for — every year.

## S6 — The lone ship (~7:05–8:25)
**Amaç / Evidence / Görsel:** v1 ile aynı (C-11, Q-03, Q-02, C-33, C-12, C-13/Q-04, C-06).

**VO:**
The auditors had a name for this. They called the Savannah a "one ship system."

The training program, the shore staff and the servicing facility existed for nuclear ships. But there was only one. So one ship carried their entire cost.

The report says the shore staff and the facility could have handled several nuclear ships, at a lower cost per ship. And the training had already produced a pool of licensed crew who could have worked on future nuclear ships.

The auditors' conclusion was simple. These costs would keep adding significantly to the Savannah's expense "as long as one vessel must absorb the full cost."

So this is what the auditors' count shows. Most of the identified extra cost came from the nuclear support system built around a single ship — a system the report says could have served several.

---

## S7 — What the audit doesn't say (~8:25–9:10)
**Amaç / Evidence / Görsel:** v1 ile aynı (Q-05, C-16, C-17, C-21).

**VO:**
It's worth being clear about what this report doesn't tell us.

The auditors say plainly that they did not review whether the experimental operation was effective. They didn't include the fuel. And the report doesn't estimate what a fleet of nuclear ships would have cost.

They also pointed to other expenses. The Savannah carried a bigger crew than comparable conventional ships — sixty-seven people, compared with roughly forty-six to fifty-five — and spent more on supplies and maintenance. They even recommended that the government stop buying one kind of insurance for the ship, to save up to seventy-three thousand dollars a year.

But the three lines are where most of the extra money went.

---

## S8 — Ending (~9:10–10:05)
**Amaç / Evidence / Görsel:** v1 ile aynı (C-23, C-25, C-24, C-22).

**VO:**
The report went to Congress as it weighed a proposal in the President's 1971 budget to lay the ship up.

According to the National Park Service, the Maritime Administration decided there was little more to gain by adding to the roughly ninety million dollars already invested in the project. The Savannah was deactivated in late 1971. Years later, she became a museum ship.

When the auditors wrote their report, no other American nuclear merchant ship had been built.

The Savannah worked. What the auditors counted was something else: the price of keeping a nuclear support system running for a fleet of one.
