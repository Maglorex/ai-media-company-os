## S1 — Cold open (~0:00–0:45)
**Amaç:** Çelişkiyi kur (çalıştı ↔ ek maliyet), denetimi tanıt, ana soruyu sor; cevabı verme.
**Evidence:** S1 kapak (V-03), C-14, C-39, C-04
**Görsel:** Siyah ekran → rapor kapağı, "JUNE 26, 1970" damgası yakın plan (V-03) · NARA renkli gemi fotoğrafı yavaş itme (V-05) · rapor özet sayfası, tek cümle vurgusu (V-02).

**VO:**
In June 1970, the auditors who check federal spending for Congress sent in a report about a single ship.

The ship was the NS Savannah — the first nuclear-powered merchant ship.

By then, she had already done what she was built to do. She had sailed hundreds of thousands of miles on nuclear power, carried freight on regular trade routes, and called at ports across Europe, the Mediterranean and the Far East.

So this isn't a story about a ship that didn't work.

It's about what it cost to keep a ship like this working — and what the auditors found when they counted.

Their answer came down to about two million dollars a year. Not a loss. Extra.

And when they traced where that extra money went, they ended up at three specific lines in their report.

To understand those three lines, we need to start with what this ship actually was.

## S2 — What the Savannah was (~0:45–2:10)
**Amaç:** Program ve geminin amacı; yolcu-kargo yapısı (nedensellik kurmadan).
**Evidence:** C-35, C-39, C-18, C-19, C-14
**Görsel:** 1955–56 zaman şeridi (özgün grafik, kaynak notu NPS) · GAO s.4 "prototype" cümlesi vurgusu · gemi profil çizimi: 7 ambar + yolcu bölümü (özgün 2D, GAO s.5 sayılarıyla) · arşiv fotoğrafları (lisans ledger'a eklenecek).

**VO:**
The idea was announced by President Eisenhower in April 1955. Congress authorized the ship the following year, and two agencies shared the job: the Maritime Administration, which handled ships, and the Atomic Energy Commission, which handled reactors.

The Savannah was completed in 1962. In the government's own words, she was a prototype — built to show the world that the United States wanted to use atomic power for peaceful purposes, and to find out whether nuclear energy was practical for commercial ships.

That double job shows in the design. She had seven cargo holds, room for about eight and a half thousand tons of freight. But she also had thirty staterooms for sixty passengers, a dining room, a lounge, a veranda — and a swimming pool.

For her first three years, from 1962 to 1965, she was mainly a demonstration. She sailed about ninety thousand miles and, in the auditors' words, helped establish an internationally accepted pattern for operating nuclear merchant ships.

Then came the real test: could she work as an ordinary cargo ship?

## S3 — It worked (~2:10–3:35)
**Amaç:** Başlığın "Worked" kısmını kanıtla; denetimin sorusuna geçiş.
**Evidence:** C-20, C-38, C-39, C-14, C-36/Q-06, C-34, C-19
**Görsel:** Sözleşme şeması (Maritime → FAST → American Export; Todd), "$1 a year" kartı · sefer haritası: rota bölgeleri (liman adı/sayısı yok) · "19 voyages / ~240,000 miles" sayaç grafiği · NPS alıntı kartı (Q-06).

**VO:**
In August 1965, the government chartered the Savannah to a subsidiary of American Export Isbrandtsen Lines, a commercial shipping company. The charter fee was one dollar a year. According to the National Park Service's history of the ship, the fuel came free of charge.

The ship became part of the company's regular cargo operations. In her first three commercial years she made nineteen voyages and sailed about two hundred and forty thousand miles — to the Mediterranean, to northern Europe, and to the Far East.

The same history says her operators called her "the most reliable ship we have operating." When she was refueled in 1968, only four of her thirty-two original fuel bundles needed to be replaced.

One thing did not make it into commercial service: the passenger spaces. The staterooms and the pool went unused, because, as the auditors noted, running them would have cost more than passengers would bring in.

So as a machine, the experiment was working. But Congress was interested in a different question.

What was all of this costing the government?

## S4 — How the auditors counted (~3:35–5:15)
**Amaç:** Yöntemi ve sınırları anlat; ~2M $'ın ne olduğunu kesinleştir. En güçlü sayfa henüz açılmaz.
**Evidence:** C-01, C-16, C-02, C-04, C-05, Q-01
**Görsel:** "Net cost" basit denklem animasyonu (cost − voyage revenue) · üç kalemin üzeri çizili "not included" kartı: depreciation / nuclear fuel / administration · GAO s.12 dipnotu vurgusu (subsidy tanımı) · iki sütunlu karşılaştırma: "Savannah, avg. per year (first 3 years) ≈ $2.9M" | "comparable conventional ships: operating subsidy" + köprü etiketi "≈ $2M more" (sübvansiyon tutarı ayrıca yazılmaz).

**VO:**
The General Accounting Office looked at the ship's commercial years, starting in August 1965.

They measured what's called the net cost: what it cost the government to operate and service the ship, minus the money she earned carrying cargo.

And they were careful about what they left out. The numbers don't include the ship's depreciation, the agency's own administration — or the nuclear fuel.

That last point matters. Nothing in this audit tells us what the uranium itself cost.

Over her first three commercial years, the net cost averaged about two point nine million dollars a year.

But a number like that means little on its own. The government also supported ordinary American cargo ships. Because American crews and repairs cost more than those of foreign competitors, the Maritime Administration paid an operating subsidy to make up the difference.

So the auditors compared the Savannah with comparable conventional ships sailing the same trade route. Their finding:

The Savannah cost "about $2 million more a year than the average operating subsidy" paid for those ships.

That two million is not a loss. It is a comparison: how much more support this one ship needed, every year, than the government already paid to keep a comparable conventional freighter on the same route.

So where did the extra two million come from?

## S5 — The three lines (~5:15–8:00)
**Amaç:** Thumbnail'deki üç satırı aç ve her birini somutlaştır (videonun kalbi).
**Evidence:** V-01, C-06, C-08, C-29, C-09, C-30, C-10, C-31, C-32
**Görsel:** **V-01 tam ekran** (thumbnail ile aynı kesit), satırlar tek tek vurgu · her kalem için: eğitim akış şeması (akademi 4–5 ay → gemi 4–5 ay → AEC lisansı), "27 of 67 crew" ikon grafiği · kara ekibi: liman giriş süreci şeması (gemi → liman → yabancı hükümet / AEC / Dışişleri) · Galveston haritası + tesis şeması (iskele, 25 akre güvenlik bölgesi, vinç, yakıt havuzu, yedek çekirdek) · yığılmış bar (300K / 409K / 1.162M; toplam etiketi GAO'nun "about $1.9 million").

**VO:**
The auditors answered that in the summary at the front of their report. Most of the extra cost — about one point nine million dollars a year — came from three things.

Line one: about three hundred thousand dollars a year for nuclear crew training.

Of the ship's sixty-seven crew members, twenty-seven needed at least some nuclear training. Reactor operators spent four to five months in classrooms at the U.S. Merchant Marine Academy, then another four to five months training aboard ship, before taking an exam for a license from the Atomic Energy Commission. By mid-1969, eighty-eight officers and engineers had earned one.

Line two: about four hundred and nine thousand dollars a year for a specially trained nuclear shore staff.

This was a team of about ten people. As the first nuclear-powered merchant ship, the Savannah was watched closely to protect her crew and the communities in the ports she visited. Every port visit took planning that an ordinary freighter didn't need — surveys, entry and departure arrangements, and in foreign ports, agreements with other governments, worked out together with the Maritime Administration, the Atomic Energy Commission and the State Department.

Line three — the biggest: about one point one six million dollars a year for a nuclear ship-servicing facility.

It was in Galveston, Texas. A pier and work area, plus about twenty-five acres of land kept clear as a safety zone. Inside: a heavy crane, a pool for spent fuel, a servicing barge called the Atomic Servant — and a spare reactor core. By the end of 1969, the government had invested about three point three million dollars in it.

And here is the detail that matters. After her first year of commercial service, the Savannah didn't come back to Galveston for two years. Her annual inspections were done in New York, with staff from the facility traveling there to help.

The facility was there, ready, and paid for — every year.

## S6 — The lone ship (~8:00–9:15)
**Amaç:** Payoff: sistemik yük; başlığın sorusunun cevabı.
**Evidence:** C-11, Q-03, Q-02, C-33, C-12, C-13/Q-04, C-08 (rezerv), C-06
**Görsel:** "lone ship system" ifadesi s.12'den vurgulu (V-belge, ledger'a eklenecek kesit) · animasyon: tek gemi ikonu, üç destek bloğunun hepsini taşıyor → aynı bloklar birden çok gemi ikonu arasında paylaşılıyor (yalnız "several"; 10 sayısı yalnız tesis için, atıflı küçük not) · Q-02 alıntı kartı.

**VO:**
The auditors had a name for this. They called the Savannah a "lone ship system."

The training program, the shore staff and the servicing facility existed for nuclear ships. But there was only one. So one ship carried their entire cost.

The report says the shore staff and the facility could have handled several nuclear ships, at a lower cost per ship. Maritime and contractor officials told the auditors the Galveston facility could easily service ten. And the training had already produced a pool of licensed crew who could have worked on future nuclear ships.

The auditors' conclusion was simple. These costs would keep adding significantly to the Savannah's expense "as long as one vessel must absorb the full cost."

So when the auditors counted the extra cost, this is what they found. Most of it wasn't the ship's own operating cost. It was the cost of the nuclear support system around her — a system that could have served several ships — paid for by just one.

## S7 — What the audit doesn't say (~9:15–10:00)
**Amaç:** Dürüst sınırlar; güven inşa et.
**Evidence:** Q-05, C-16, C-17, C-21
**Görsel:** Rapor sayfaları üzerinde üç kısa "not in this report" kartı · basit mürettebat karşılaştırma grafiği (67 vs ~46–55).

**VO:**
It's worth being clear about what this report doesn't tell us.

The auditors say plainly that they did not review whether the experimental operation was effective. They didn't include the fuel. And they didn't calculate what a fleet of nuclear ships would have cost.

They also pointed to other expenses. The Savannah carried a bigger crew than comparable conventional ships — sixty-seven people, compared with roughly forty-six to fifty-five — and spent more on supplies and maintenance. They even recommended that the government stop buying one kind of insurance for the ship, to save up to seventy-three thousand dollars a year.

But the three lines are where most of the extra money went.

## S8 — Ending (~10:00–11:00)
**Amaç:** Hikâyeyi kapat; açılış sorusuna kısa son cevap.
**Evidence:** C-23, C-25, C-24, C-22
**Görsel:** GAO s.3 "lay up" cümlesi · 1971 zaman şeridi · bugünkü/arşiv gemi fotoğrafı (lisansı ledger'a eklenecek) · son kare: V-01 üç satır + V-05 gemi, kapakla aynı kompozisyon.

**VO:**
The report went to Congress as it weighed a proposal in the President's 1971 budget to lay the ship up.

According to the National Park Service, the Maritime Administration decided there was little more to gain by adding to the roughly ninety million dollars already invested in the project. The Savannah was deactivated in late 1971. Years later, she became a museum ship.

When the auditors wrote their report, no other American nuclear merchant ship had been built.

The Savannah worked. What the auditors counted was something else: the price of keeping a nuclear support system running for a fleet of one.
