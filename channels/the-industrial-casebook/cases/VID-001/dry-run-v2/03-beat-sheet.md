# VID-001 — Creative Treatment / Beat Sheet (dry-run)

> author: COO main session (Stage 3 dry-run, 2026-10-14) · date: 2026-10-14 · status: DRAFT · skill: `documentary-creative-treatment` · inputs: `01-story-viability.md`, `02-packaging-hypothesis.md`, P3 evidence ledger

## Central question
"If America's nuclear cargo ship worked, why did running it cost about $2 million a year more than comparable conventional ships?"
(Türkçesi: Amerika'nın nükleer kargo gemisi çalıştıysa, işletmesi neden benzer konvansiyonel gemilerden yılda yaklaşık 2 milyon dolar daha pahalıya geldi?)

## Structure
Paradox → investigation (dossier opening): the audit is the case file; each line on the page becomes a thing the viewer can see. Opening logic: PARADOX / QUESTION (B-01–B-02) → CASE ENTRY (B-03) → CASE INTRO position (B-03; not designed until calibration) → EVIDENCE TEASE (B-04) → INVESTIGATION (B-05 onward).

## Beats
### B-01 — It worked
- **Story Function:** BREAK ASSUMPTION, NEW CURIOSITY
- **Viewer Question / Curiosity State:** "A nuclear merchant ship from the 1960s — wasn't it a failure?"
- **Claim:** The ship operated: about 90,000 miles and 55 ports in its demonstration phase, 19 voyages in its first three commercial years; its operators called it "the most reliable ship we have operating".
- **Evidence Anchor:** C-14, Q-06 (NPS attribution), V-05, V-19
- **Payoff / Next Question:** Closes "did it work?" (yes) and opens "then what is this story about?"
- **Earns screen time because:** It breaks the assumption the viewer brings and makes the coming cost paradox surprising instead of expected.

### B-02 — The audit's number
- **Story Function:** NEW CURIOSITY, BRING EVIDENCE
- **Viewer Question / Curiosity State:** "If it worked, what is the problem?"
- **Claim:** In 1970 federal auditors found it cost about $2 million a year more than the average operating subsidy paid for comparable conventional ships on the same trade route.
- **Evidence Anchor:** C-04, Q-01, V-02, V-03
- **Payoff / Next Question:** Sets the central question: where did that extra money go?
- **Earns screen time because:** This is the paradox the packaging sells; without it the video has no question to answer.

### B-03 — Case entry
- **Story Function:** NEW CURIOSITY
- **Viewer Question / Curiosity State:** "Whose file is this, and what are we opening?"
- **Claim:** none — framing beat
- **Evidence Anchor:** none — framing beat
- **Payoff / Next Question:** Frames the investigation (CASE 001 · NS SAVANNAH); CASE INTRO position reserved, not designed before Golden Master calibration (TIC-11).
- **Earns screen time because:** It turns a list of costs into an investigation the viewer is invited into, and anchors the channel identity briefly.

### B-04 — Three lines on a page
- **Story Function:** BRING EVIDENCE, SET UP PAYOFF
- **Viewer Question / Curiosity State:** "Is it the reactor? The crew? Something else?"
- **Claim:** By the auditors' own total, about $1.9 million of the extra came from three nuclear-specific items: crew training, specialised shore staff, and a nuclear servicing facility.
- **Evidence Anchor:** C-06, V-01
- **Payoff / Next Question:** Narrows the question to three items; opens "what are these, physically?"
- **Earns screen time because:** It is the turning point from mystery to investigation and teases all three answers without spending them.

### B-05 — Not the fuel
- **Story Function:** BREAK ASSUMPTION
- **Viewer Question / Curiosity State:** "Surely the expensive part is the nuclear fuel?"
- **Claim:** The audit's cost figures exclude the cost of the nuclear fuel; the extra is something else.
- **Evidence Anchor:** C-16
- **Payoff / Next Question:** Removes the obvious answer; sharpens "so what costs extra if not fuel?"
- **Earns screen time because:** It pre-empts the viewer's most likely wrong guess and keeps the investigation honest about what the numbers include.

### B-06 — People who had to learn the reactor
- **Story Function:** HUMAN DIMENSION, NEW INFORMATION
- **Viewer Question / Curiosity State:** "Why would training cost so much?"
- **Claim:** Nuclear crew training averaged almost $300,000 a year; 27 of the 67 crew had to be nuclear trained, reactor operators spending months at an academy and months aboard.
- **Evidence Anchor:** C-08, C-29, V-15
- **Payoff / Next Question:** First item explained; opens the second: who supported the ship on shore?
- **Earns screen time because:** It gives the first cost a human face and makes an abstract line item concrete.

### B-07 — The shore team
- **Story Function:** HUMAN DIMENSION, EXPLAIN SYSTEM
- **Viewer Question / Curiosity State:** "What does a ship need on land?"
- **Claim:** A specially trained nuclear shore staff of about ten people cost about $409,000 a year — port surveys, entry arrangements, inspections beyond what a conventional ship needed.
- **Evidence Anchor:** C-09, C-30, V-16
- **Payoff / Next Question:** Second item explained; points to the biggest one: a facility.
- **Earns screen time because:** It reveals that the ship's cost lived partly outside the ship, which is the seed of the payoff.

### B-08 — A facility in Galveston
- **Story Function:** SHOW SCALE, EXPLAIN SYSTEM
- **Viewer Question / Curiosity State:** "What could cost over a million a year for one ship?"
- **Claim:** The nuclear servicing facility at Galveston, Texas cost about $1,162,000 a year: a service barge (Atomic Servant), a 75-ton gantry crane, a spent fuel pool, a spare reactor core, and an exclusion zone.
- **Evidence Anchor:** C-10, C-31, C-42, V-13
- **Payoff / Next Question:** Biggest item explained; opens "how often did the ship actually use it?"
- **Earns screen time because:** It is the largest share of the answer and the strongest visual opportunity: infrastructure the viewer has never seen.

### B-09 — Two years away
- **Story Function:** BREAK ASSUMPTION, HUMAN DIMENSION
- **Viewer Question / Curiosity State:** "Surely the ship was there all the time?"
- **Claim:** The ship did not return to the facility for two years, until August 1968; the 1967 and 1968 annual inspections took place in New York and facility staff travelled there.
- **Evidence Anchor:** C-32
- **Payoff / Next Question:** Shows a system sized for constant work waiting for one ship; opens "why was it built like that?"
- **Earns screen time because:** It turns a cost line into an image of waiting, which makes the mechanism felt before it is explained.

### B-10 — One ship carrying the system
- **Story Function:** EXPLAIN SYSTEM, CONNECT IDEAS, SET UP PAYOFF
- **Viewer Question / Curiosity State:** "Was the facility simply too big?"
- **Claim:** The auditors described a "one ship system": as long as one vessel must absorb the full cost, these costs keep the operation expensive; the shore staff and facility could serve several nuclear ships at lower cost per ship.
- **Evidence Anchor:** C-11, Q-02, Q-03, C-12, C-33, V-12
- **Payoff / Next Question:** Connects all three items into one mechanism; sets up the answer.
- **Earns screen time because:** It is the mechanism that makes every earlier beat mean something.

### B-11 — The answer
- **Story Function:** PAYOFF
- **Viewer Question / Curiosity State:** "So why did it cost more?"
- **Claim:** The extra cost came from a nuclear support system built around a single ship — not from the ship failing to work. By 1970 no other nuclear merchant ship had been built in the U.S.; the ship was deactivated at the end of 1971 (per NPS).
- **Evidence Anchor:** C-11, C-27 (INFERENCE — careful wording), C-22, C-24
- **Payoff / Next Question:** Closes the central question; leaves a quiet image of the ship today.
- **Earns screen time because:** It delivers the "so that's what it was" moment the packaging promised and closes the opening question.

## Risks
Differentiation (see story viability); C-27 must stay inference ("the audit points to…"); fleet size must stay "several" (D-018); Galveston facility has no photo — reconstruction must use only C-31/C-42 components.
